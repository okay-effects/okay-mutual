# Questions

**Is this a cryptocurrency?**
No. There is no coin, no token and no price. It uses the same building blocks — keys, signatures, records chained
by fingerprints — to keep honest books of who owes what, and nothing else.

**Can I buy units, or sell them for money?**
No, and that is the point. Units appear when a member buys and disappear when that member sells. Nobody issues them
and nobody can stockpile them past their ceiling.

**Then what is a unit worth?**
What its community can supply. A unit counts as one unit of the national currency, so prices are familiar, but it
can only be spent with members. A network where you can buy bread, a haircut, bookkeeping and repairs is worth a
great deal; one with nothing you need is worth nothing. This is why a community starts dense — one town, one
trade — rather than wide.

**Does it replace money?**
No. It runs beside it. Prices stay in the national currency, a deal can be part units and part money, and taxes,
rent and fuel go on being paid in money. See [the idea](idea.md#beside-money-not-instead-of-it).

**Do I pay tax on a sale for units?**
Yes. A sale for units is a sale at its ordinary price; VAT and income tax apply as for money. Barter networks that
advertised themselves as a way around tax were shut down, rightly. What the network does give you is a signed
record of every deal, so the bookkeeping is already done. Ask a tax adviser about your own case.

**What if someone runs up a debt and leaves?**
Their loss to the community is at most their credit line, which was small until they had earned it. If someone
vouched for them, the voucher's own room carries the loss. A community can also keep a fund, fed by membership
fees paid in money, to cover what is left. No software can make a person honest; limits keep dishonesty cheap.

**Why is there a limit on how much I can earn?**
Because your positive balance is other members' negative one. If you only sell and never buy, the others run out of
room and trade stops. The ceiling asks you to spend — which is how the others earn. Hoarders break mutual credit as
surely as debtors do.

**Is there interest?**
None, on either side. A negative balance costs nothing but the duty to earn it back; a positive one earns nothing.

**Who controls it?**
Each community controls its own node: whom it admits, what limits it gives, what fee it charges. No one controls
the network as a whole. The protocol and the reference software are open; anyone can run a node or write another.

**Who pays for running a node?**
Its community, in money: a membership fee, as in WIR or Sardex. A node costs little to run. Fees in units would be
a hidden way of issuing them, so a node does not take them.

**Is my data public?**
No. A chain holds amounts and fingerprints. Names, contact details and what a payment was for are kept beside it,
seen by you, the other party and your node, and can be erased. Other members see your public name and nothing of
your balance.

**What if I lose my phone?**
Another of your devices cuts it off. If you lose them all, the people you named for recovery, or your node, can add
a new key after an openly announced waiting period that any surviving device can cancel.

**Can private people take part, or only businesses?**
Both. A community decides whom it admits, and the usual choice is: businesses, the self-employed, and private people
whose accounts never go below zero. A business may buy before it has sold, within its credit line. A private person
earns first — by work or by something sold to a member, or by units a business gives — and then spends what was
earned. That keeps the system out of the rules on credit to consumers, and it is how such networks have brought
private people in before. A community may instead admit only businesses, or give private people credit lines too.

**Can my family or my company share an account?**
Yes: an account can be held by several people's accounts, each with its own limit, and can require two signatures
for large amounts. A department's or a child's budget is an account with a line lent from the parent's.

**Does it work on a phone?**
The app is a web page that installs like an app. Keys are made and kept by the phone's own secure storage.

**How is it different from Sardex or WIR?**
The economics are the same and proven. The difference is that those run on one operator's books. Here every member
holds their own keys and can check the books, anyone can start a community, and communities connect to each other.

**How is it different from network marketing?**
Nobody earns from recruiting, there are no levels and nothing to buy in order to join. Bringing someone in costs
you part of your own credit line until they have proved themselves.

**Is it legal?**
Mutual credit systems operate lawfully in many countries, including in the European Union. How rules on payment
services, electronic money and taxes apply depends on the country and on how a node is set up, and must be checked
with a lawyer before a community opens to people it does not know. We list the open questions in
[the spec](../specs/mutual.md#questions-for-a-lawyer-and-a-tax-adviser).

**What exists today?**
See the [README](../README.md): it separates what runs from what is designed.

**How do I start a community?**
Not with software. Find ten to twenty people who each have something the others need, keep the books in a
spreadsheet for a month, and see whether units actually circulate: whether people sell for them, find something to
spend them on, and bring others. If they do, the software is ready to take over the bookkeeping.
